# Examples: real paragraphs, rewritten in the voice

Each "before" is from version one of the site (2026-11-04). Each "after" keeps every fact and adds
nothing that isn't sourced; check it against the case study's research file before using it.
They're calibration, not final copy: UV approves the voice.

## Portal: the problem (rule, reason, plain words)

**Before:**
> Collections agencies reach people by letter, SMS and phone. To pay, the person who owes usually
> has to call back or wait for a call, often during working hours, about something they'd rather
> not discuss.
>
> The agency carries those calls, and every payment that needs a person costs staff time. Before
> the portal, all the agency had online was the collections platform's stock page. If someone
> wanted to pay, it gave them a phone number, and the call went to the back office or to a voice
> agent.

**After:**
> Owing money is hard enough. Paying it shouldn't need a phone call. But agencies reach people by
> letter, text and phone, and to pay you usually had to call back or wait for a call, often in
> office hours, about something you'd rather not discuss.
>
> Those calls cost the agency too: every payment that needed a person took staff time. Online, all
> it had was the collections platform's stock page. If you wanted to pay, it gave you a phone
> number, and the call went to the back office or to a voice agent.

Why it works: the empathy opens with a plain statement instead of an adjective, and the "too" in
the second paragraph links the person's cost and the agency's in one beat. Every fact in the
"before" survives: all three channels, "or wait for a call", "often", where the call went. A
rewrite that drops one of those, or adds a judgement the sources don't make ("and not much
else"), has broken the first rule however good it sounds.

## Reflect: the stakes (a number made human)

**Before:**
> So the agency has to be able to see what the AI did, judge it and challenge it, and Rifa's own
> QA team has to do the same first. Nobody can read it all: there have been over 100,000 chats so
> far, and the voice agents made about 240,000 calls in the last 30 days alone.

**After:**
> So the agency needs to see what the AI did, judge it, and push back when it's wrong, and Rifa's
> QA team has to get there first. The catch: nobody can read it all. There have been over 100,000
> chats so far, and the voice agents made about 240,000 calls in the last 30 days alone.

**Margin note:** 100,000 chats is a lot of bedtime reading

Why it works: "The catch:" turns a list into a problem; the joke is about the size of a true
number, not about anyone.

## rifa.ai website: version one (honest about what broke)

**Before:**
> The first version of rifa.ai, which I designed and built in Framer in 2025, tried. A "Talk to
> Rifa AI" button started a live call with Rifa's own agent. It broke in front of visitors (see
> Designing for model limits). After that, recordings were all a visitor could hear, and talking
> to the agent meant booking a call with sales.

**After:**
> My first rifa.ai, built in Framer in 2025, had a button that put you on a live call with our
> agent. It broke in four ways. Visitors asked random questions, asked for a human, or never
> allowed the microphone, and the agent, not ready yet, answered with delays and silences. So
> version two played recordings instead. Recordings never stumble. They also never answer back.

**Margin note:** four ways to break a demo, all found live

Why it works: the four failures are UV's own account, and each is put on whoever caused it (the
silences were the agent's, not the visitors'). The two short sentences at the end name the
trade-off of recordings with a smile, and set up version three.

## Coozy: what I'd do differently (a concession that sounds human)

**Before:**
> Parents live in the milestone cards and leap alerts, so I'd bring the AI into those moments,
> with a "why is this happening?" on a leap or a milestone, instead of keeping it in its own tab.
>
> I'd also give activities more of the content budget. "Activities get repetitive" and "would be
> nice to have different activities every 2 days" were the next most common complaints after
> price.

**After:**
> Parents lived in the milestone cards and leap alerts. The AI lived in its own tab. I'd move it
> to where the parents already were: a "why is this happening?" on the leap or the milestone
> itself.
>
> I'd also give activities more of the content budget. After price, the most common complaint was
> that activities got repetitive. Fair: they're the part parents open every day.

**Margin note:** the AI was the supporting act; the content was the band

Why it works: the parallel "Parents lived… The AI lived…" makes the problem visible before the fix;
"Fair:" concedes like a person, not a report.

## Things that would break the rules

- "Built in a week; still live two years on." Joins two versions: the week was the next version's
  prototype (Sep-Oct 2025); what's been live since early 2025 is v1. And "two years" is 21-22
  months.
- "There was no payment page before." The agency had the collections platform's stock portal page;
  UV's build replaced it.
- "Our team spent sleepless nights perfecting the flow." Invented scene.
- "Users loved it!" No source, an exclamation mark, and a claim instead of evidence.
- "Unlike [named competitor]'s clunky checkout…" Names and mocks a company.
- "Debtors can finally stop dodging calls." Laughs at the people the product serves.
- "A seamless, delightful journey that empowers users." Four words from the flatten list.
