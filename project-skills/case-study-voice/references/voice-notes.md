# Voice notes: how the reference essay works

A close reading of a designer's essay on the values behind their product (read 2026-11-04), described in our
words. It's here to explain the techniques, not to be copied: none of its sentences, analogies,
coined phrases or jokes go into UV's pages.

## Shape

- **Title:** a pun on the product's name that also states the subject (the values behind the
  product). The last line comes back to the same pun, with a margin note owning up to it.
- **Opening, four short moves:** what the product is and how long he's worked on it; why he's
  writing now (other designers had started recreating its components); what the essay isn't (not
  a tutorial, with credit to people he calls better at that); and a small, honest admission that
  part of the point is to keep a record of work he's proud of.
- **Principles up front:** three named principles, each defined in a single sentence, then
  restated as what they do for the person using the product.
- **One section per principle,** each headed in the same pattern (a principle, then the method
  that delivers it), so the reader always knows where they are.
- **Each method explained as a system with rules:** what triggers it, how it behaves, the
  constraint that keeps it honest. Almost every rule is followed by its reason, and a clip shows
  it.
- **Everyday analogies,** one at a time, for an abstract idea: rooms seen through a doorway,
  moving through water, flying versus teleporting, a smart restaurant with a neglected bathroom.
  Each is tied straight back to the interface.
- **With and without:** he removes his own polish for a demo (and says it was never shipped), so
  the reader feels what the design adds. One comparison uses another company's product, with an
  apology in brackets. (We don't name other companies.)
- **A coined concept** with a deliberately rough diagram: a curve relating how often a feature is
  used to how much delight it should get.
- **Trade-offs near the end:** the approach slowed the launch; the basics (utility, performance,
  security) mattered just as much and get named as table stakes.
- **Close:** a belief about what good design does, then one short line that calls back to the
  title.
- **Acknowledgements and footnotes:** everyone who built the interactions, everyone who read
  drafts, and the people and essays that inspired ideas.

## Sentences

- Mostly medium sentences in plain words, with an occasional short one that lands the point.
- One-line paragraphs for emphasis, used sparingly.
- Rule, then reason, then example, again and again; it's the backbone of the essay.
- "We" for the team's decisions, "I" for opinions, definitions and pet peeves.
- Respect for the person using the product is stated plainly and often: their time, their
  attention, their feelings.
- Effort is admitted, not hidden: the work was obsessive and sometimes tedious, and the essay says
  so.

## Humour

- **Where:** almost all of it sits in hand-written margin notes. The body stays sincere; the
  margins wink. About seven notes across 4,300 words; with the asides in brackets, one light
  moment every 400 to 500 words.
- **The kinds of note it uses:** a self-correction that inflates its own number; a mock-dramatic
  warning before a with-and-without demo; credit to the friend who supplied a joke; a countdown
  before a deliberately rough diagram; a fond exception to a general claim; a shout-out to the app
  that inspired an interaction; a promise to keep making puns.
- **In the body:** a few short asides in brackets (gratitude, a confession that something wasn't
  shipped, an apology to a team being compared), and one or two vivid comparisons.
- **Never:** sarcasm, contempt or punching down. The jokes are about the writer, the work and
  everyday life.

## Borrow and leave

| Borrow | Leave |
| --- | --- |
| Rule, reason, example | His sentences, analogies, coined phrases and jokes |
| Principles named early and returned to | The essay's length: about 4,300 words, nearly four times the reference portfolio's |
| Humour kept in the margins | Naming and comparing other companies' products |
| With and without, to show what a decision adds | Spelling that isn't the case study's own |
| Honesty about effort and trade-offs | Footnotes (the site has none; credit inline or in "In short") |
| Generous, specific credit | Delight as a claim about your own work: show it instead |
| A title pun and a callback last line, when natural | Forcing a pun |
